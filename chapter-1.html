Chapter One

It Begins

CRIMSON TRAILS · M.L.

The rain in Veridian didn't wash things clean. It merely turned the grime into a liquid shadow that clung to the heels of those in a hurry.

Inside the office, the air smelled of expensive sandalwood and old books. Dr. Tirian Maxwell sat in his high-backed leather chair, his posture perfect, his expression a masterpiece of professional calm. His hands were folded over a notebook, the image of a man who had spent his life absorbing the pain of others without allowing it to stain him.

In the corner, the television was muted, but its flickering blue light pulsed through the dim room. A static image of a twenty-five-year-old girl filled the screen. Bright eyes. A graduation cap. Beneath her face, the headline read: MISSING STUDENT FOUND DEAD IN CREEK; POLICE SEEK LEADS.

Tirian's gaze remained fixed on the man sitting across from him. He didn't look at the news. In fact, he seemed entirely unbothered by the tragedy unfolding just a few feet away.

Mark Thorne was twenty-two, but he carried himself with the ancient, unearned confidence of a king. He was currently picking an invisible piece of lint from his three-thousand-dollar trousers, his lip curled in a permanent expression of distaste.

He looked at the world as if it were a dirty window he was forced to look through.

"My mother thinks I have 'anger issues'," Mark said, his voice dripping with the kind of boredom that only comes from extreme wealth. He finally glanced at the muted news report and shrugged. "I think people just have a habit of being in my way. It's not my fault they're fragile."

Tirian leaned back, his chair letting out a soft, compliant groan. He clicked his pen once. The sound was sharp, almost clinical, in the soundproofed room.

"Fragility is a subjective term, Mark," Tirian said, his voice as smooth as silk. "In this room, we don't look at fault. We look at patterns. Tell me about the first time you realised that your actions had... permanent consequences for someone else."

Mark laughed, a sharp, cold sound that didn't reach his eyes.

"Permanent? You sound like my father's lawyers. They're always talking about mitigating damage." He shifted in his seat. "I just want to know when I can leave. I have a flight to catch on Friday."

Tirian smiled. It was a small, reassuring gesture. He opened a fresh page in his notebook. He didn't write a name. He simply drew a small, precise circle, perfect and empty.

"We have plenty of time," Tirian murmured.

"Let's start with the money. You mentioned it feels like a shield. Tell me, Mark... what are you so afraid is going to hit you?"

Mark didn't answer. He simply stared at the small, empty circle in Tirian's notebook as if trying to find a hidden insult within the ink. Finally, he checked his gold watch and stood.

"Time's up, Doctor. I've got dinner at the Shard," Mark said, his voice regaining its oily confidence. "Same time next week? Assuming my mother hasn't found a 'better' way to spend her money."

Tirian stood, offering a hand that Mark ignored.

"Patterns, Mark. They always repeat until they are broken. I'll see you next week."

Tirian watched him walk out, his footsteps echoing in the hallway. Only when the heavy oak door clicked shut did he allow his smile to fade.

He walked to the window, watching Mark's silver sports car roar away into the Veridian rain. He looked like a scientist observing a virus under a microscope, completely focused on the structural flaws of his patient's mind.

The Maxwell penthouse was a sanctuary of soft textures and warm light, a sharp contrast to the cold precision of the office. As Tirian stepped inside, the smell of burnt toast and vanilla greeted him.

Elara was curled up on the sofa, the glow of her laptop screen illuminating the tired shadows beneath her eyes. She didn't look up when he entered. Her phone was clutched in her hand like a lifeline or a weapon.

"He still hasn't replied, Ti," she whispered, her voice cracking.

Tirian shed his coat, hanging it up with practised care. He walked over and sat at the edge of the sofa, a warm, protective expression settling over his features.

"The writer? The one with the 'logical' mind?"

Elara nodded, blinking back tears.

"I sent him the poem. The one about the void. I thought... I thought he was different. But it's been six hours. Am I just invisible?"

Tirian reached out, smoothing a strand of hair away from her face. His touch was gentle, his protective instincts instantly flaring at the sight of her distress. Every tear she shed was an unnecessary disruption to the order he kept around her.

"You are a Maxwell, Elara," he said softly. "You see the essence of things. Most people are only capable of seeing the surface. If he cannot see you, it isn't because you are invisible. It's because he is blind."

"It just hurts," she murmured, leaning her head against his shoulder. "To be so open and get nothing back."

Tirian held her, his gaze drifting to the window, where the city lights of Veridian twinkled like cold diamonds. The clinical detachment of his day job melted away into pure, familial defence. He didn't know the name of the man who was making his sister cry yet, but he knew one thing for certain.

He was going to find out. And once he did, he would use his understanding of human nature to show this man exactly what it felt like to be dismissed.

"Go to sleep, Elara," he whispered. "The world will look different in the morning. I'll take care of everything."

He stayed there long after she fell asleep, her head still resting on his shoulder. Her phone lay on the coffee table, the screen still open to the unsent draft of a poem she'd titled The Glass House. It was about the fear of being seen too clearly and then being shattered by the view.

Tirian reached out and picked up the device. He didn't read her private thoughts to be intrusive; he read them to understand the shape of her pain. As his eyes skimmed the lines, his jaw tightened.

He wasn't a man who believed in letting things go. He believed in structural balance. If someone had tipped the scales against his sister, it was his job as her older brother to correct them.

He wouldn't hurt this boy. But he would find him. He would sit him down, man to man, and explain exactly what happened to people who treated a Maxwell like a temporary distraction. Tirian was a master of words, after all. He knew how to break a person's psychological defences without ever raising his voice.

He set the phone back down, his analytical mind already beginning to map out a profile of the man who had caused this.

He couldn't bear seeing his sister hurt.

Because while the world saw Dr. Tirian Maxwell as a healer, his sister was the only one who knew him as her shield. And a shield's only purpose was to take the hits so the person behind it didn't have to.
